# Decision Log

Non-obvious decisions made while building this, and why. (Obvious ones —
"use Python," "use pandas for CSVs" — are omitted.)

1. **BM25 over embeddings for retrieval grounding.** At the scale of one
   brand's historical resolved cases (thousands, not millions), BM25 is
   fast, needs no vector DB or extra embedding API calls, and is trivially
   explainable/debuggable in a live interview ("why did it retrieve this
   example — because these 4 words overlap"). Embeddings would likely
   generalize better to paraphrase-heavy real data (Failure mode 5 in the
   report), but that upside didn't justify the added infrastructure and
   reduced explainability for this scope. Implemented a pure-Python BM25
   fallback (`src/retrieval.py::_MiniBM25`) so the repo doesn't hard-fail if
   `rank_bm25` isn't installed, since I couldn't `pip install` it in the
   sandbox this was built in and wanted a testable code path regardless.

2. **Escalation logic is rule-first, LLM-second, not LLM-first.** A binary
   safety decision needs to be auditable and reproducible run-to-run. Rules
   over concrete signals (dollar amounts via regex, security keywords,
   classifier confidence, weak retrieval grounding) are all things you can
   point to in a log and say "this is exactly why it escalated." The LLM is
   used for exactly one signal that's genuinely hard to regex — anger/
   distress level — and even that only pushes the decision one direction
   (toward escalating), never away from it.

3. **The escalation rule is deliberately biased toward escalating, not
   balanced.** A false "auto_handle" (should have escalated) is a much
   worse failure than a false "escalate" (should have auto-handled) — the
   former can mean a fraud report or billing dispute gets a canned reply
   and silently drops. I chose to accept a higher false-escalation rate as
   the cost of higher escalation recall. This is a business-risk judgment
   call, not a metric-optimal one, and it's stated explicitly rather than
   buried in a threshold.

4. **`account_access_issue` is *always* escalated, full stop, regardless of
   classifier confidence or anything else.** This is a hard-coded policy
   exception, not a learned threshold. Account security is the one category
   where I decided no model confidence level justifies auto-handling,
   because the downside (helping an attacker, or mishandling a real
   compromise) is asymmetric and severe enough to not want any model
   discretion here at all.

5. **8 intents, not more, not from Banking77.** I open-coded a sample of
   real customer messages, merged near-duplicates, and collapsed
   sub-4%-frequency categories into "other." I considered borrowing
   Banking77's 77-label taxonomy (offered as an optional secondary dataset)
   but rejected it — it's a retail-banking taxonomy, and forcing e-commerce
   logistics/billing traffic into it would mean either a lossy many-to-one
   mapping or half the 77 labels never firing. Fewer, brand-appropriate
   intents that map to genuinely different reply strategies and escalation
   risk profiles were more useful than a bigger, borrowed label set.

6. **Reply drafting is explicitly instructed not to fabricate facts, and
   the escalation rule treats the drafter's own "I might have fabricated
   something" signal as an escalation trigger.** This double-checks the
   same risk two ways (prompt instruction + self-report used downstream)
   rather than trusting the prompt instruction alone, because prompted
   instructions to "not hallucinate" are necessary but not sufficient.

7. **Confidence and anger thresholds (0.6, 0.7) are stated as guesses, not
   tuned values**, and I say so explicitly in the report rather than
   presenting them as validated. I'd rather show an honest, cheap threshold
   than a fake-precise one I didn't actually sweep.

8. **The golden set's escalation labels are generated by the same rule
   function that `escalation.py` uses (`gold_escalate_rule` mirrors
   `decide_escalation`'s logic).** This is a real limitation I call out
   directly in the report: it's a self-consistency check on the *policy*,
   not an independent human judgment. I chose to be explicit about this
   circularity rather than dress it up as blind human labeling, and flagged
   it as the top priority to fix with real data (report Section 5, item 1).

9. **Reply quality judge uses 4 axes, not more.** More rubric axes without
   more human-labeled ground truth to validate them against just adds judge
   noise you can't distinguish from signal. I picked the 4 axes I could
   actually defend as distinct and gradeable (helpfulness, tone fit,
   faithfulness, actionability) rather than a longer list that looks more
   thorough on paper.

10. **Judge-vs-human agreement uses within-1-point agreement as a headline,
    not just exact match**, because on a 1-5 rubric even two careful human
    raters routinely disagree by 1 point, and treating that as "wrong" would
    make every judge look unreliable regardless of quality. Exact match is
    still reported alongside it, and a judge that's frequently off by 2+
    points should be considered disqualifying.

11. **Trivial baseline always escalates (not: never escalates, not: random
    50/50).** I picked "always escalate" as the trivial/safe default
    because it's the policy a genuinely risk-averse team might actually
    ship on day one before any modeling — it's a real baseline someone might
    propose, not a strawman. This also makes its escalation-recall=1.0
    meaningful context for interpreting the real system's recall (Section 2
    of the report).

12. **Simple baseline gets real templated replies with brand voice, not
    literally "we got your message"** — I wanted the simple baseline to be
    a fair fight (a competent engineer's keyword-and-template afternoon
    project), not a weak strawman, so the intent-vs-LLM and reply-quality
    comparisons are meaningful rather than trivially won.

13. **Synthetic sample data ships in the repo, clearly labeled as such, with
    the real-data path unchanged.** I considered shipping no sample data at
    all (forcing the grader to download Kaggle's data before anything runs)
    versus shipping a synthetic stand-in. I chose the latter so the "under
    15 minutes to reproduce" requirement is actually satisfiable without a
    multi-GB download blocking the first run, while being loud (README, this
    log, module docstrings, in-code comments) that it is not a substitute
    for evaluation on real data.

14. **Lexical grounding (token overlap with the historical reference reply)
    is reported alongside the LLM judge score, even though it's a weak
    metric.** It costs zero extra API calls and catches one specific
    failure mode cheaply: a drafted reply that's grounded in nothing at all
    related to how the brand actually responds. It's a floor check, not a
    quality metric, and the report doesn't conflate the two.

15. **Single-message decisions, no thread reconstruction**, even though the
    raw data has `response_tweet_id`/`in_response_to_tweet_id` links that
    could support it. Thread reconstruction on real Twitter support data
    has its own serious data-quality problems (broken chains, customers
    starting fresh threads instead of replying) that deserved dedicated
    time I didn't have. Scoping it out explicitly (Report Section 1) felt
    more honest than a half-working multi-turn feature.
